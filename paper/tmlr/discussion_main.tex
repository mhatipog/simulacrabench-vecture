On a real survey, three design decisions account for most of the gain over our earlier ensemble: stacking on out-of-fold predictions of all visible rows instead of a small holdout, stacking the gate and the answer separately, and letting weights vary by question with shrinkage. Each is cheap and none depends on a particular expert. The experts themselves are interchangeable to a degree that surprised us: on GSS only CatBoost and the dedicated gate models are individually significant, and the neighbour experts and empirical-Bayes tables could be dropped. Where the neighbour experts matter they matter a great deal, but only when scored rows share structure with visible rows; when that structure exists among visible rows alone, out-of-fold stacking is fooled. We therefore recommend comparing out-of-fold skill with any held-out estimate available (a leaderboard, or a split grouped by a suspected unit) as a routine diagnostic, and grouping folds by any unit that the scored rows cannot share. The development leaderboard could not adjudicate any of these choices (\S\ref{sec:leaderboard}), so the final selection rested on paired offline evidence.
